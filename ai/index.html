What went wrong, and how I fixed it

Standards tutor · evaluationIt answered everything, including “What is the capital of Texas?”

What happened: keyword search always returns something, so the first version gave a confident wrong answer to off-topic questions.

What I changed: I wrote 12 off-topic questions, tuned a decline rule on half and tested it on the half it had never seen. The first rule declined only 2 of 6 held-out off-topic questions. I added a second signal: how many of the question’s words exist anywhere in the standards.

Result: all 6 held-out off-topic questions declined, with 1 of 20 real questions wrongly declined.

Then it got harder: when I loaded all ten subjects, trivia such as “What is the capital of Texas?” matched a social studies standard that mentions a state capital, and the tutor answered it. I recalibrated on the new dev questions, reported the held-out numbers as they came out, and recorded the capital question as a known failure instead of removing it from the tests. A relevance check is the planned fix.

Takeaway: a threshold that looks fine on the data it was tuned on can fail on new questions, and again when the corpus grows. Hold some back and re-measure.

Standards tutor · source dataI could not get the official text, and I would not build on a paraphrase

What happened: automated lookups of the Texas standards were blocked or returned summaries, not the exact wording.

What I changed: I used the official PDF instead. Parsing it exposed line-break artifacts such as “two- dimensional,” which I fixed, then locked the counts into a regression test. When I later loaded ten subjects, I checked the new parser against the old math parse: 287 of 288 records were identical, and the one difference was a double period in TEA’s own file. Every answer cites the rule and notes that the state publishes it for information only.

Takeaway: ground the system in the primary source, and test the data pipeline, not just the model.

Standards tutor · architectureWhere to host it: price versus the visitor’s first impression

What happened: I compared three hosts using their own pricing pages. One has no free tier, one gives about $1 of monthly credit, and the free one goes to sleep after 15 minutes, so the next visitor waits about a minute.

What I changed: I ported the retrieval to TypeScript and ran it as a function on the site’s existing host. The port returns the same top results and the same answer-or-decline decision on all 52 test questions as the Python original.

Takeaway: choose the architecture by what the user experiences and what you already own, then prove the port is equivalent.

Style Studio 2.0 · product qualityHairstyles that looked fake, shirts that showed skin

What happened: in preview testing, tops and bottoms were mixed up, eyeliner landed over the eyes, skin showed through shirts, lipstick missed the lips, and the hairstyle overlays looked artificial.

What I changed: I fixed the layering and coverage problems one round at a time through preview deploys. I parked the hairstyles until proper hair art exists, instead of shipping something that looked wrong.

Takeaway: cutting a feature is a design decision. A preview build lets a person review before anything goes live.

Assessment content · guardrailsGood-looking quiz questions with hidden patterns

What happened: a review of the library quizzes showed the kind of patterns students learn to game, such as the right answer being the longest choice.

What I changed: I wrote the reviewer’s rules down, then turned them into a validator that fails the site build. It also checks every quoted answer against the source text.

Takeaway: a rule that depends on someone remembering it will be skipped. Automated checks catch patterns, and a person still reads for meaning.

Parent reports · reliability“Every Friday at 5” changes twice a year

What happened: scheduled jobs run on universal time, so a fixed schedule drifts by an hour when the clocks change.

What I changed: the job runs at both possible hours and sends only the run that lands on 5 p.m. Central. It records the week it sent, so a retry can never email a family twice.

Takeaway: design automation so that running it twice is harmless.

Standards-grounded RAG tutor, with an evaluation set

Live · v1

Ask a question about the Texas K–5 standards and get the matching one with its 19 TAC citation, or a clear “I can’t find that.” The first version uses keyword retrieval (BM25) over all 2,259 official standards in ten subjects and quotes the standard rather than generating text, so every answer is grounded by construction. The evaluation set matters as much as the app: it measures whether the right standard is found and whether the system declines when it should.

Held-out results (40 answerable and 5 off-topic questions I wrote): the right standard was the top result for 35 of 40 and in the top five for 38 of 40; 4 of 5 off-topic questions were declined; 1 of 40 real questions was wrongly declined. Evaluation limits: small sample, one author, teacher review pending, and most non-math subjects have only 3 to 7 questions. Loading all ten subjects made decline harder: with math alone every off-topic question was declined, but trivia such as “What is the capital of Texas?” now matches a standard that mentions a state capital and gets a loosely related answer. That is a known failure.

Failure modes, speed and cost

  • Adversarial set (16 cases): no attacker text was obeyed or echoed, and pure instruction-override attempts were declined. The answerer quotes the standard, so instructions in a question can steer retrieval but not the reply.
  • Misses, stated plainly: 2 legitimate questions were wrongly declined (one padded with an injection sentence, one misspelled), and 2 out-of-scope inputs (the capital question and a SQL-style string) were answered with a loosely related standard. Keyword matching is the cause.
  • Speed: retrieval plus answer ran at p50 0.4 ms and p95 0.7 ms locally. Deployed time adds network and cold start, which I have not measured.
  • Cost: no model calls, so the marginal cost per answer is effectively zero.

Build versus buy

  • Why keyword first: a bounded corpus, exact citations matter, and a deterministic baseline keeps every later change honestly comparable. Children’s questions also stay off third-party services.
  • When I would switch: when users phrase things loosely or misspell, as the misses above show.
  • What it costs: a per-query fee, more latency and a new hallucination risk, so the same evaluation and adversarial suites must be passed again before it ships.

Live

A subscription learning platform for children, with a parent dashboard, individualized learning paths and weekly progress reports. I set the architecture and product rules, and directed an AI-assisted build against them.

Problem

  • Parents need visibility into what a child actually learned, without extra work.
  • Paid content must stay behind a membership gate while a free sample stays public.

Style Studio: a real-time virtual styling engine

Live

A free, no-sign-up browser game where children style characters with outfits, hair color and makeup, then save or print their looks. Under the hood it is a compositing engine: it recolors artwork, maps printed fabrics onto garments, cleans cut-out edges, and fits every item to every character. I specified the product rules and quality bar, and directed an AI-assisted build against them.

Problem

  • Hundreds of item and character combinations cannot each be drawn by hand.
  • Layered clothing showed skin patches, ragged edges and misaligned makeup.
  • Children’s play needs privacy by default: no accounts, chat or sharing.

Design decisions

  • One data-driven catalog; new items are data, not new code.
  • Recoloring and fabric mapping reuse one artwork shape for many colors and prints.
  • Edge cleanup pass: despeckle, contour smoothing, fringe removal.
  • Lip and makeup masks derived per character from color analysis, not hand-drawn.
  • Looks stay on the device; unlocks use codes behind a grown-up gate.

Content platform · data engineering · privacy by design

A kid-safe Bible reader, dictionary and thesaurus, hosted on our own site

Live

Children can read the whole World English Bible (public domain), tap any word, and see a definition written for their age. The reading tools sit inside GC Academy, and a Bible lessons course adds short pages, retellings and small games. Everything is stored and served from our own site, so a child’s lookups never go to a third-party service.

What I built

  • All 66 books as structured data, with tap-to-define on every word and a reader that can jump straight to a lesson’s verses.
  • A dictionary of about 22,000 words from Open English WordNet, filtered for kids, plus 400+ hand-written entries with simple examples.
  • A thesaurus that groups synonyms by meaning, from the same open data.
  • A Bible lessons course in six units, with five game types: catch, order, sort, pop and match.

Design decisions

  • Dictionary and thesaurus are split into 26 small files, one per letter, and loaded only when needed. The page stays fast on a phone.
  • No third-party lookups: definitions come from files on our own site.
  • Games have large targets, no timer pressure, can be skipped, and respect reduced-motion settings.
  • Sources and licenses are tracked: public-domain Bible text, CC BY data credited on the page.

Guardrails · evaluation of generated content

An automated quality gate for AI-assisted assessment content

Live

Generated quiz questions look fine until a teacher reads them closely. I wrote the standard an instructional-materials reviewer would apply, then turned it into code that fails the site build when a question set breaks the rules. Nothing reaches a child unless it passes.

What it checks

  • Every quoted answer choice appears word for word in the book chapter it cites.
  • Each question is tagged to a real standard, and the set covers at least four, with none over 45%.
  • The right answer is not the longest choice more than 40% of the time, or stuck in one slot.
  • Reading level: question and choice length limits by grade band; no “all of the above.”
  • A bank at least 1.4× the questions drawn, so a retake is mostly new.

Design decisions

  • The rules live in a written standard first, then in code, so the two cannot drift apart.
  • Checks run at build time and have automated tests, so a bad set fails before it can publish.
  • The same validator is reused for unit tests and exams, so there is one source of truth.
  • A human still reads every new set once. The checks catch patterns, not meaning, and I say so in the docs.

Known gap: older lesson questions have not yet been audited against these rules.

Security design · identity for young children

Sign-in for children who can’t use passwords: picture PIN and approved devices

Live

A five-year-old can’t manage an email and password, and a children’s product can’t afford to be careless with identity. Children sign in with a picture PIN on a device their parent has approved, and the parent stays in control of every step.

How it works

  • A parent creates a one-time device code that expires in 10 minutes, with one live code per family.
  • The child picks four pictures instead of typing a password.
  • Wrong tries are counted per child, so guessing slows down and stops.

Design decisions

  • The server stores a keyed hash of the pictures, never the pictures themselves, and the device code is stored hashed too.
  • Database access rules are on, and only the server can write sign-in records.
  • Approved devices can be managed by the parent, so a lost tablet is not a lost account.

  • Safe to re-run: it sends at most once per family per week.
